How ground_truth.json numbers were actually picked

Being fully honest with you: I wrote them by hand, using directional reasoning, not from any dataset or citation. Here's exactly the logic I used, so you know precisely what you're standing on:

hard_decline → retries almost always fail (0.01–0.02), escalation is decent (~0.55–0.62): this is genuinely well-established - a hard decline (stolen card, closed account, etc.) means the card itself is dead; retrying the same card at a different time doesn't fix that. This one I'm fairly confident is directionally correct even without hard data.
insufficient_funds → retry_72h scores highest (0.6–0.72): based on the common industry heuristic that salary/payday cycles mean funds often become available a few days later. This is a genuinely used heuristic in dunning-management literature (Chargebee, Recurly blog posts reference this pattern), but I didn't verify the exact numbers - I picked "72h clearly better than 2h/24h" as the shape, and invented the specific decimals (0.65, 0.72, etc.) myself to make the pattern visible in a small dataset.
timeout and issuer_unavailable → shaped around "how long does a transient outage typically last": this is closer to a guess dressed up as a heuristic. I made timeouts recover better after a delay (systems catch up) and issuer_unavailable recover faster (short blip, retry soon) - reasonable-sounding, but I have no source backing the specific numbers.
Bank-to-bank variation (HDFC vs SBI vs ICICI etc. differing slightly): this part is entirely fabricated by me, purely to give your bandit something bank-specific to discover (since your pitch's headline claim is "different bank behavior gets learned separately"). There's no real basis for HDFC recovering faster than SBI in my numbers - I made that up so the demo has visible bank-level structure to show off.

So the honest framing, if a judge asks "where do these numbers come from": "These are illustrative synthetic parameters, directionally grounded in known payment-industry patterns (payday cycles, hard-decline behavior, transient outages), not real per-bank retry data - which isn't publicly available to us. The point of the Bayesian bandit design is precisely that it doesn't need these starting numbers to be exactly right; it corrects itself against real observed outcomes over time. In production, this table would be replaced by real historical retry-outcome data." That's a legitimate, common answer for synthetic-data hackathon demos - you're not claiming secret access to real bank statistics, you're claiming a defensible mechanism that works regardless of whether the starting guess is perfect.


Research findings (grounding our caps):

- UPI Autopay (NPCI, Aug 2025 rules) - 1 original attempt + 3 retries max per mandate cycle. This is the most concretely published Indian rail-level rule, and it's literally cited on Razorpay's own blog.

- Visa's Excessive Reattempts Rule - soft declines (insufficient funds, timeouts, system errors) get up to 15 reattempts in a 30-day window; hard declines (lost/stolen/closed card) get zero retries, ever - retrying those is an automatic violation.

- Mastercard - stricter, commonly cited around 10 retries in 30 days, plus it signals per-transaction via Merchant Advice Codes (MAC 03 = do not retry at all, MAC 24-30 = specific wait windows).

- RuPay - no separately published numeric cap found; since NPCI governs RuPay too, I'll conservatively apply the same NPCI-style cap as UPI Autopay, flagged clearly as an assumption in the code, not a confirmed figure.





"Why is there a hard rule engine instead of letting the model learn it?"

This is a design decision, not a limitation, and here's the crisp version for Q&A:

The core argument: asymmetric stakes. A bandit optimizes expected value, and to keep learning, it has to occasionally explore actions that look suboptimal - that's not a bug, it's the mechanism. The cost of exploring a slightly-worse retry timing is small (a missed conversion). The cost of exploring past a network's retry cap is a compliance violation - potentially fines, and at volume, risk toward a merchant ban. When the downside is that asymmetric and that irreversible, you don't want "usually respects the limit" - you want "never violates it, provably." A learned policy can't give you a guarantee; a deterministic rule can.

This isn't something we invented - it's a known pattern. In robotics and safe-RL literature this is called "shielding" or "action masking": a learned policy operates freely within a safety envelope that's enforced by a separate, deterministic layer wrapping it. Self-driving systems, algorithmic trading risk limits, and industrial control systems all use this same separation - the model doesn't get veto power over hard safety constraints, no matter how well-trained it is.

And the audit log data backs up that this isn't dead weight: from the smoke test we just ran, 81% of decisions were the bandit's own proactive choice - it usually learns to escalate before the rule engine ever has to step in (thanks to the risk-adjusted reward we built). The rule engine only intervened in the remaining ~19%, mostly cold-start/exploration cases. So the honest framing is: the bandit does most of the work; the rule engine is the guarantee for the part a bandit structurally can't guarantee on its own.

"Is this actually multi-bandit? Where are the multiple bandits?"

Good catch to make sure this is defensible, because the name deserves scrutiny. Here's the precise answer:

Technically, "multi-armed bandit" already refers to one decision-maker choosing among multiple arms - that's the standard term, arms plural, bandit singular. What we built is a step beyond that: we don't have one bandit with 4 arms - we have 20 independent bandits, one per (bank, error_code) segment, each with its own 4 arms and its own separately-evolving Beta posteriors. Look at SegmentedThompsonBandit.posteriors - it's a dict keyed by segment, and HDFC's timeout bandit shares zero learned state with SBI's insufficient_funds bandit. They don't know about each other. That's genuinely multiple bandits, running in parallel, each specializing in its own corner of the problem - which is exactly what "reMultiBands" describes.

How do they "work together" if they don't share learning? Two ways:

Shared mechanics, not shared state - every segment's bandit uses the identical update rule (risk-adjusted, revenue-weighted Thompson Sampling), so they're all "the same kind of learner," just running independently per segment, the way 20 separate students learn the same subject from the same textbook but form their own individual understanding.
Shared governance - a single RuleEngine instance sits on top of all of them, enforcing the same network-cap and stopping-rule logic uniformly, regardless of which segment's bandit is making the call underneath. So the coordination isn't in the learning - it's in the compliance layer that governs every bandit the same way.

If a judge pushes on "so why not one shared model," the honest answer is: sharing state across segments would mean HDFC's behavior contaminates SBI's estimate, which is precisely the mistake plain epsilon-greedy makes (and the thing we built segmentation specifically to avoid) - different banks and different decline types genuinely behave differently, so keeping them independent is the point, not an oversight.



We use an additive approximation for network effects today because it's the right complexity for the data volume we have. In production, we'd monitor for segments where that assumption breaks down and let those specific segments graduate to independent tracking once they've earned enough data to support it - same self-correcting philosophy as the rest of the system, just applied to model structure instead of just posterior beliefs."
